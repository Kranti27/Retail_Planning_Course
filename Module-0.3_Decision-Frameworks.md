## Executive Summary & Core Dilemma

How do planning executives actually make decisions? Not what tools they use or what metrics they watch — but what strategic mental frameworks guide their thinking to protect shareholder return when they face ambiguous situations with imperfect information?

---

## Why Decision Frameworks Exist

### The Problem: Every Planning Decision is Made Under Uncertainty

When you allocate 500 units across 80 stores, you're making predictions that impact enterprise capital:
- "Store #12 will sell 8 units per week" 
- "This product is in Growth stage" 
- "This demographic segment prefers modern designs"

When David decides to markdown a slow-moving line, he's predicting:
- "At 20% off, velocity will increase enough to clear stock"
- "Not marking down NOW destroys more value later"
- "The markdown cost is less than the holding cost of trapped capital"

Every one of these predictions carries financial risk. Decision frameworks help you mitigate risk and maximize expected enterprise value.

### The Trap: Over-Reliance on Gut vs. Over-Reliance on Data

Experienced planners sometimes trust gut too much. They can't explain their reasoning to the C-suite and can't adapt when market conditions change.
Analytical planners sometimes trust data blindly. When the model's assumptions are wrong, they fail to catch the error, destroying capital.

**The best executives use both:** Data-informed intuition. Decision frameworks are the bridge to strategic alignment.

## Framework 1: The 2×2 Trade-Off Matrix

### What It Is

The simplest and most useful strategic framework. When you face a trade-off between two competing objectives, map them on a 2×2 grid to visualize risk.

### How It Works: The Availability vs. Investment Trade-Off

```
                    HIGH AVAILABILITY
                          │
        ┌─────────────────┼──────────────────┐
        │                 │                  │
        │  QUADRANT 2     │  QUADRANT 1      │
        │  Overstocked    │  Ideal State     │
        │  High stock,    │  Right stock,    │
        │  high in-stock  │  high in-stock   │
        │  but capital    │  efficient       │
        │  inefficient    │  capital use     │
        │                 │                  │
LOW  ───┼─────────────────┼──────────────────┼─── HIGH
INVEST  │                 │                  │  INVEST-
MENT    │  QUADRANT 3     │  QUADRANT 4      │  MENT
        │  Lean & risky   │  Worst state     │
        │  Low stock,     │  High stock but  │
        │  low in-stock   │  still out of    │
        │  but low cost   │  stock on key    │
        │                 │  items (wrong    │
        │                 │  mix)            │
        └─────────────────┼──────────────────┘
                          │
                    LOW AVAILABILITY
```

**Quadrant 1 — Ideal:** Capital is deployed efficiently, maximizing yield.
**Quadrant 2 — Overstocked:** High availability but at the cost of too much capital. Destroys ROIC.
**Quadrant 3 — Lean & Risky:** Low investment and low availability. Starves revenue.
**Quadrant 4 — Worst Case:** Capital is deployed heavily but availability is STILL low (wrong mix). This was likely Sarah's diagnosis before her strategic inventory restructuring.

## Framework 2: Expected Value Thinking

### What It Is

Every decision is a bet with shareholder capital. Expected Value (EV) helps evaluate bets by quantifying probability.

**Expected Value = Σ (Outcome_i × Probability_i)**

### How It Works: The Markdown Timing Decision

David has 2,000 units of a slow-selling line. It's Week 10 of a 16-week season. 1,300 units remain. He has two options to minimize margin destruction:

**Option A: Markdown NOW at 20% off**
- Revenue at 20% off (MSRP $100 -> $80):
  - Good response (600 units, 70% prob): $48,000
  - Weak response (400 units, 30% prob): $32,000
  - **Weighted EV: $43,200**
- Remaining units: ~760 still in stock.

**Option B: Wait 3 more weeks, then markdown at 30% off**
- Full-price sales in 3 weeks (EV): ~180 units at $100 = $18,000
- Remaining (1,120 units) marked down to $70:
  - EV revenue from markdown (assuming 50% response): $39,200
  - **Total EV Option B: $57,200**

While Option B has a higher EV for revenue, it leaves more residual stock (560 units) that may require deeper write-offs. The executive must balance immediate EV against terminal value risk.

## Framework 3: Reversible vs. Irreversible Decisions

### What It Is

Not all decisions are equal. The amount of analysis you need is proportional to the irreversibility and capital at risk.

**One-Way Door (Irreversible):** Committing $5 Million to next season's buy.
**Two-Way Door (Reversible):** Allocating 50 units to Store #23.

Spend your analytical energy on one-way doors. For two-way doors, decide quickly, execute, and adjust.

## Framework 4: Constraint-Based Thinking

### What It Is

Instead of asking "What's the best plan?" ask "What are the structural constraints, and what's the optimal strategic plan given these constraints?"

```
HARD CONSTRAINTS
├── Budget (Capital is finite)
├── Physical capacity (Store sqft)
├── Sourcing lead times

SOFT CONSTRAINTS
├── Margin targets
├── Option count

ASSUMED CONSTRAINTS
├── "We always allocate to all 250 stores"
```

**The powerful question:** "Is this a real constraint or an assumed constraint limiting enterprise value?"

## Framework 5: The Regret Minimization Framework

### What It Is

When facing a decision with uncertain outcomes, ask: **"Which option destroys less value if I'm wrong?"**

### How It Works: Over-Buy vs. Under-Buy

| Decision | If Demand = 800 | If Demand = 1,500 |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Buy 1,000 | 200 excess (~$X markdown loss) | 500 missed sales (~$5X lost revenue) |
| Buy 1,500 | 700 excess (~$3.5X loss) | Perfect — no regret |
| Buy 2,000 | 1,200 excess (~$6X loss) | 500 excess, some markdown |

In most retail scenarios, the opportunity cost of lost sales exceeds the cost of a markdown, except for end-of-life products where excess becomes a total write-off.

## Framework 6: Pre-Mortem Analysis

### What It Is

Instead of analyzing what went wrong AFTER failure, **imagine the strategic decision has already failed** and work backward to figure out WHY, mitigating risks proactively.

1. **Assume failure.** "It's 16 weeks from now. We missed target by 20%."
2. **Identify causes.** What broke?
3. **Adjust the plan.**

Sarah's inventory restructuring was likely preceded by a pre-mortem identifying exactly what would break if they dropped holding to 8 months.

---

## Framework 7: First, Second, and Third Order Effects

### What It Is

Strategic decisions have ripple effects. First-order effects are obvious. Second and third-order effects are often invisible but can destroy long-term value.

**Decision:** Reduce allocation to bottom 50 stores to concentrate stock in top 200.
- **1st Order:** Top 200 stores have deeper stock (Positive)
- **2nd Order:** Bottom 50 stores have empty shelves (Negative)
- **3rd Order:** Brand perception in those markets degrades, permanently hurting market share (Strategic Damage)

---

## Framework 8: The OODA Loop — Observe, Orient, Decide, Act

### What It Is

The fastest decision cycle for agile in-season management to protect capital.

```
OBSERVE → ORIENT → DECIDE → ACT → (loop)
```

The planner who runs this loop weekly makes 52 course corrections per year. Speed is a competitive advantage.

## Framework 10: The Ladder of Inference

A mental model for understanding how you jump from data to conclusions, avoiding bias.

```
ACTION          "Send 200 units to NY stores"
    ↑
CONCLUSION      "NY stores need more stock"
    ↑
ASSUMPTION      "High footfall means high demand"
    ↑
INTERPRETATION  "NY stores have the highest footfall"
    ↑
SELECTED DATA   "NY footfall = 8,000/week"
```

**Where can this go wrong?** Data Selection Bias, Assumption Error. Walk down the ladder to find the weak rung before committing capital.

## Executive Perspectives

### How Sarah Thinks About Frameworks
Sarah operates in the realm of Pre-Mortems, 3rd Order Effects, and Irreversible decisions. Her focus is entirely on removing structural risks from Aura Global Retail's enterprise strategy.

### How David Operates on Frameworks
David relies heavily on Expected Value and the OODA Loop. He ensures the tactical decisions—markdowns, replenishments, OTB adjustments—are mathematically sound and rapidly executed to maximize yield.

---

## Strategic Trade-Offs & Risk Matrices

- **Speed vs. Accuracy:** The OODA Loop prioritizes speed of course correction, while Expected Value modeling demands more upfront accuracy. An executive knows when to switch modes based on capital at risk.
- **Intuition vs. Data:** Data models ignore qualitative market shifts; intuition ignores statistical probabilities. True leaders balance both.

---

## Strategic & Operational Pitfalls

1. **Analysis Paralysis:** Treating a two-way door allocation like a one-way door OTB commitment.
2. **Anchoring on Last Year:** Last year is a reference point, not a strategy.
3. **Sunk Cost Bias:** Refusing to markdown a failed line because of the heavy initial capital investment.
4. **Survivorship Bias:** Attempting to repeat a success by copying just one variable.
5. **Recency Bias:** Altering a full season strategy based on one bad week of sales.

3. **Identify Assumed Constraints:** Question 5 rules you follow to see if they are truly hard constraints limiting enterprise value.

## Key Takeaways

1. Decision frameworks bridge the gap between blind intuition and over-reliance on rigid data models.
2. The 2×2 Trade-Off Matrix identifies areas needing structural intervention to improve ROIC.
3. Expected Value (EV) helps quantify decisions involving probability, such as markdown timing.
4. Time spent on a decision should be proportional to the capital at risk (One-Way vs Two-Way doors).
5. Pre-Mortems catch preventable failures before they destroy enterprise value.
