## 1. Executive Brief & Operating Dilemma

**Date:** February 10, 2028  
**Context:** Aura Global Retail Assortment Review.  
**The Dilemma:** The buying team just received 500 units of a highly anticipated, premium Silk Bedcover. The junior planner executed a "democratic allocation": 500 units divided by 250 stores = 2 units per store. 

The result was a catastrophe. The 10,000 sqft flagship in Downtown London sold its 2 units by 11:00 AM on launch day and spent the rest of the month stocked out, losing thousands in potential revenue. Meanwhile, the 1,500 sqft boutique in a Tier-3 transport hub received its 2 units, shoved them in the backroom because there was no shelf space, and sat on them for a year until they were marked down 80%.

Treating unequal stores equally is the fastest way to destroy margin. You cannot manage 250 stores individually. You must mathematically group them. This is the science of Grading and Clustering.

---

## 2. Competency Targets (Bloom's Taxonomy)

Upon completing this module, you will be able to:

4. **Evaluate** Assortment Truncation limits to prevent over-assorting C-tier stores.

---

## 3. Theoretical Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

### 3.1 The Two Dimensions of Store Analysis
1. **Grade (The Y-Axis):** Driven by *Volume*. Answers: **"How MUCH stock?"** Determines inventory *Depth*. (e.g., A, B, C, D).
2. **Cluster (The X-Axis):** Driven by *Profile*. Answers: **"WHAT KIND of stock?"** Determines inventory *Width*. (e.g., Metro, Tier-2, Tourist).

### 3.2 Assortment Truncation Math
If the total assortment is 200 options:
- **A-Grade:** Gets 100% of the range (200 options).
- **B-Grade:** Gets the top 70% (140 options).
- **C-Grade:** Gets only the top 40% (80 core options).

## 7. Post-Mortem: How Retailers Fail

### Autopsy: The Static Grading Disaster
A national retailer graded their 500 stores in 2023. 
In 2025, a new metro line opened next to a previously sleepy 'C-Grade' store, tripling its daily footfall. Because the Planning Department only updated grades annually, the system continued feeding the store C-Grade (shallow) allocations. 
The store sold out of its weekly deliveries on Monday mornings, operating with empty shelves for 4 days a week. The retailer lost an estimated $1.2 Million in uncaptured revenue over 6 months before someone realized the store grade was wrong. 
**The Lesson:** Grades must be dynamic, recalculated automatically at the end of every quarter based on a trailing 12-month rolling average.

# Module 4.2: Allocation — First Distribution of Stock

**Semester:** 4 — The Movement  
**Prerequisites:** Module 4.1 (Store Grading & Clustering), Module 3.3 (Availability & Service Level)  
**Estimated Study Time:** 6-7 hours  
**Level:** Core

---

## 1. Executive Brief & Operating Dilemma

**Date:** March 2, 2028  
**Context:** Aura Global Retail Fall Launch Allocation.  
**The Dilemma:** You have 1,500 units of a highly anticipated ceramic dinner plate. You have 250 stores. 

The junior allocator executing the push decides to "spread the peanut butter," allocating exactly 6 units to every single store to ensure everyone gets a piece of the launch. 

The VP of Planning immediately intercepts the shipment. 
Dinner plates are bought by consumers in sets of 4, 6, or 8. If you send 6 plates to a flagship store, one customer will buy the set at 10:00 AM, and the flagship will be out of stock for the rest of the launch week. If you send 6 plates to a C-Tier store, they will sit there for a year.
Allocation is the spatial disaggregation of risk. You must concentrate depth where it will sell, even if it means starving the bottom tier of stores entirely. 

---

## 2. Competency Targets (Bloom's Taxonomy)

Upon completing this module, you will be able to:
1. **Deconstruct** the Push (Allocation) vs. Pull (Replenishment) mechanics.
2. **Formulate** Initial Allocation using the Target WOC and Display Minimum equation.
3. **Architect** Master Carton and Inner Pack rounding algorithms to protect supply chain margins.
4. **Synthesize** a Warehouse Hold-Back strategy to mitigate risk on unproven trends.

---

## 3. Theoretical Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

### 3.1 The Allocation Equation
Used for the initial "Push" of a new item before sales history exists.
`Initial Allocation = (Target WOC × Expected Weekly ROS) + Display Quantity`
- *Expected Weekly ROS* = Chain Average ROS × Store Grade Index.
- *Target WOC* = Desired weeks of cover (usually Lead Time + 2 weeks).

### 3.2 Minimum Viable Depth (MVD)
The absolute floor quantity that justifies shipping. If the Allocation Equation yields a number lower than the MVD or Display Minimum, the store receives **zero**. Breadth is sacrificed to protect Depth.

### 3.3 Master Carton Optimization
Never break factory seals unless necessary. Breaking cartons spikes DC labor costs and breakage rates.
- High Volume Store: Round UP to nearest Master Carton.
- Low Volume Store: Round DOWN to nearest Inner Pack.

---

**The Scenario:** Proxy SKU Allocation
You are launching a brand new Geometric Print Bedcover. It has no sales history. 

**The Execution:**
1. Identify the Proxy: You pull the data from last year's Floral Print Bedcover launch (same price tier, same season).
2. Proxy Chain ROS: The Floral cover sold 3 units/week/store on average.
3. Calculate Store Target: Store A (Grade Index 1.5) expects 4.5 units/week.

## 7. Post-Mortem: How Retailers Fail

### Autopsy: The 100% Push Disaster
A fast-fashion brand received 10,000 units of a highly experimental neon jacket. Confident in the trend, the planner executed a 100% Push, emptying the warehouse and distributing all 10,000 units across the network.
Reality hit: The trend only resonated in urban coastal cities. Inland stores sold zero. Coastal stores sold out in 3 days. 
Because the warehouse was empty, the planner could not replenish the coastal stores. To fix it, they had to execute Inter-Store Transfers (shipping from inland stores to coastal stores), destroying the margin in freight costs and losing weeks of peak selling time.
**The Lesson:** Never Push 100% of an unproven trend. Push 70%. Hold 30% in the DC to "Read and React" to actual localized demand in Week 2.

# Module 4.3: Replenishment — Keeping Stores Fed

**Semester:** 4 — The Movement  
**Prerequisites:** Module 4.1 (Store Grading), Module 4.2 (Allocation), Module 3.3 (Availability)  
**Estimated Study Time:** 6–7 hours  
**Level:** Core

---

## 1. Executive Brief & Operating Dilemma

**Date:** April 8, 2028  
**Context:** Aura Global Retail Store Operations Review.  
**The Dilemma:** A C-Grade store manager is furious. A highly experimental, expensive decor piece was allocated to his store. It didn't sell a single unit for 3 weeks (Rate of Sale = 0). In Week 4, a customer finally bought the solitary display unit. 

The next day, the Replenishment Engine automatically shipped another unit to the store to "replace" it. 

The manager complains: "It took me a month to get rid of that dud, and the system immediately sent it back!" 
The naive replenishment algorithm functioned perfectly based on its programming (replenishing back to the Display Minimum). But commercially, it was a disaster. Replenishment is the automated engine that pulls cash from the enterprise. If the math is wrong, it will fund failure at scale.

---

## 2. Competency Targets (Bloom's Taxonomy)

Upon completing this module, you will be able to:
1. **Quantify** True Rate of Sale (ROS) by filtering out stockout distortion.
2. **Formulate** the Model Stock equation balancing Lead Time Demand and Display Minimums.
3. **Architect** a "Replenishment Fuel" hold-back strategy to fund pull demand.
4. **Evaluate** the Bullwhip Effect caused by ignoring Stock In Transit (SIT).

---

## 3. Theoretical Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

### 3.1 Push vs. Pull Mechanics
- **Allocation (Push):** Based on guesswork, forecasting, and Store Grades.
- **Replenishment (Pull):** Based on actual localized consumer demand. 

### 3.2 The Replenishment Engine
`Order Quantity = Model Stock - Current Inventory Position`
- *Current Inventory Position* = Stock on Hand (SOH) + Stock In Transit (SIT). 
- *Model Stock* = `max(Display Minimum, Lead Time Demand + Safety Stock)`

### 3.3 True ROS vs Naive ROS
`Naive ROS = Total Sales / Total Weeks` (Danger: penalizes fast-sellers that stocked out).
`True ROS = (Total Sales / In-Stock Days) × 7` (Rewards actual velocity).

## 6. Step-by-Step Operational Modeling Lab

**The Scenario:** The Model Stock Calculation
You are setting the replenishment algorithm for a Brass Lamp in the London Flagship. 
- *True ROS:* 4 units/day.
- *Lead Time (DC to Store):* 3 days. 
- *Safety Stock Requirement (calculated via 95% Z-score):* 10 units.
- *Visual Merchandising Display Minimum:* 4 units.
- *Current SOH:* 6 units. *SIT:* 12 units. 

**The Execution:**
1. Lead Time Demand (LTD) = 4 units × 3 days = 12 units.
2. Model Stock = max(4, 12 + 10) = 22 units. 
3. Current Position = 6 (SOH) + 12 (SIT) = 18 units.
4. Order Quantity = 22 - 18 = **4 units**. 
*The system automatically drops a pick-ticket in the DC for 4 units.*

---

## 7. Post-Mortem: How Retailers Fail

### Autopsy: The Bullwhip SIT Disaster
A junior planner built a basic min/max replenishment script in Excel, but they forgot to include the **Stock In Transit (SIT)** variable in the 'Current Inventory Position' calculation. 
- Store Target was 50 units. 
- SOH was 10. The script ordered 40 units on Monday. 
- On Tuesday, the SOH was still 10 (the 40 units were on a truck). The script triggered *another* 40 units.
- On Wednesday, the script triggered *another* 40 units. 
By Friday, 120 units arrived at a store that only needed 40. The backroom overflowed into the fire exits, and the company trapped $80,000 in dead stock in a single week. 
**The Lesson:** Never write a replenishment algorithm that ignores the supply chain pipeline. SIT is as real as SOH.

# Module 4.4: Space Planning & Planogramming — The Physical Constraint

**Semester:** 4 — The Movement  
**Prerequisites:** Module 4.1 (Store Grading), Module 4.2 (Allocation), Module 4.3 (Replenishment)  
**Estimated Study Time:** 6-7 hours  
**Level:** Core

---

## 1. Executive Brief & Operating Dilemma

**Date:** May 10, 2028  
**Context:** Aura Global Retail Assortment Delivery.  
**The Dilemma:** A planner built a beautiful Open-to-Buy model, predicting massive growth in the Home Decor category. They expanded the assortment to 150 unique SKUs and shipped 10 units of each to a C-Tier mall store. 

The Store Manager calls in a panic. The store only has three physical fixtures designated for Home Decor, which can hold a maximum of 80 SKUs. 

The remaining 70 SKUs (and their 700 units of depth) are immediately shoved into the backroom. They never see the sales floor. They rot for 6 months, age past 180 days, and are eventually marked down by 70%, destroying the category margin. 

Simultaneously, a flagship store with ample space is constantly stocked out because the buying budget wasn't aligned with their vast fixture capacity. Space is the ultimate physical constraint on retail mathematics, but it must be seamlessly connected to buying, allocation, and replenishment to avoid trapping capital. 

---

## 2. Competency Targets (Bloom's Taxonomy)

Upon completing this module, you will be able to:
1. **Deconstruct** space productivity by calculating true Sales Density, Unit Stock Density, and Margin Density (GMROF).
2. **Formulate** Assortment Width ceilings based on Planogram (POG) fixture capacity and Presentation Minimums.
3. **Diagnose** space productivity issues using combined signals (e.g., high sales density + low stock density) rather than relying on a single KPI.
4. **Synthesize** Space-to-Sales Share realignments and prioritization strategies for inventory-constrained chains.

---

## 3. Theoretical Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

### 3.1 Space Capacity and Presentation Constraints
- **Selling Area vs. Total Store Area:** Always use *selling area* for density calculations. Do not dilute metrics by including backrooms.
- **Physical Capacity vs. Commercial Need:** The physical capacity of a fixture (what it *can* hold) often differs from the commercial need (what demand dictates). 
- **Presentation Minimums:** The minimum display stock required to make a fixture look shoppable, separate from demand-driven stock depth. 

### 3.2 Space Productivity Densities
Do not claim that "density" has one universally standardized definition. Define the metric clearly:

- **Sales Density (SPSF):** `Net Sales for the period / Selling Area`. (e.g., $15/sq ft/month). High sales density supports comparison across differently sized stores but doesn't necessarily mean a store needs more space.
- **Unit Stock Density:** `Inventory Units on Hand / Selling Area`. Differentiate between display stock and backroom stock.
- **Inventory Value Density:** `Inventory Value at Cost / Selling Area`. 
- **Margin Density (GMROF):** `Gross Margin Generated / Selling Area`. Categories with different sales and margin rates produce different commercial outcomes. 

### 3.3 Integration with the Retail Planning Pipeline
Space planning is not a silo. It is an iterative cycle:
**Store Role → Fixture Constraints → Assortment (Width) → Presentation Minimum → Demand (Depth) → Buying Constraints → Allocation/Replenishment → Performance Review.**
- *Assortment (Module 1.2):* Space dictates feasible width.
- *Buying (Module 1.3):* The buy must fund presentation minimums and demand.
- *Allocation & Replenishment (Modules 4.2 & 4.3):* Quantities must be validated against fixture capacity without confusing minimums with total stock requirements.

| The Signal | Diagnostic Meaning | Recommended Executive Action |
|---|---|---|
| **High Sales Density + High Stock Density** | Highly Productive. | Check availability, stock turn, and margin. Ensure inventory pipeline is sufficient to maintain momentum. |

## 7. Post-Mortem: How Retailers Fail

### Autopsy: The Inventory-Constrained Chain
A national chain with 200+ stores built a perfect space-productivity model. They determined exactly how much inventory each store physically needed to fill its fixtures. 
However, their buying budget was slashed by 30% due to a cash flow crisis. The planner ignored this and tried to spread the constrained inventory to meet the "presentation minimums" of all 200 stores.
The result: Every store received a microscopic layer of inventory. No store looked full. A-Tier flagship stores stocked out in two days and lost their top customers. C-Tier stores held onto stagnant units they couldn't sell.
**The Lesson:** Central warehouse capacity and space plans do not override buying realities. If the buy plan is constrained, the binding constraint is supply, not space. You must prioritize scarce stock using Store Tiers (Module 4.1) and Demand, not blindly filling every fixture equally.
