## Introduction: Why Study Retail Planning?

### The Invisible Profession That Runs a $500 Million Business

Walk into any retail store — a sprawling department store, a boutique fashion label, or a global home décor outlet — and you will see visual merchandisers arranging displays, store managers greeting customers, and marketing teams running campaigns. What you will never see is the person who decided that *this exact product* should exist on *this exact shelf* in *this exact quantity* at *this exact moment in time*.

That person is the Retail Planner. And they are, arguably, the single most important function in the entire retail value chain for generating shareholder return.

Here is a truth that most retail professionals never learn: **the fate of every retail company is decided 6 to 9 months before the customer walks through the door.** It is decided in a planning room, on a spreadsheet, in a meeting where someone committed $5 Million of the company's capital to a collection of products that may or may not sell. If that person made the right strategic call, the company thrives and maximizes return on invested capital. If they got it wrong — if they bought too many units, or the wrong sizes, or bet on a trend that died — the company spends the next two quarters bleeding cash through markdowns, dead inventory write-offs, and emergency liquidation, eroding enterprise value.

There is no marketing campaign that can save a bad buy. There is no visual display that will move a product the customer does not want. There is no store manager who can manufacture demand out of thin air.

**The buy is the business. And the planner controls the buy.**

### The Skills Gap

Despite this, retail planning remains one of the most under-taught and under-documented disciplines in the business world. Top-tier MBA programs cover corporate strategy and financial modelling, but almost none teach the mechanics of Open-To-Buy, the mathematics of Sell-Through curves, or the art of reading an inventory aging report to protect gross margin. Most planners learn their craft through years of on-the-job trial and error — often at the cost of millions in avoidable operational mistakes.

This curriculum exists to close that gap.

## The Philosophy of Retail Planning

Before you can study retail planning, you must understand what it actually is. 

**The Misconception:** Planning is the administrative task of looking at a spreadsheet and moving boxes of product.
**The Reality:** Planning is the ruthless, mathematical allocation of a company's working capital to maximize shareholder return. 

Your sole objective as a planner is to velocity-test your investments: converting purchased goods back into liquid cash (plus a profit margin) as fast as the laws of physics and consumer demand will allow.

Every module in this university is anchored to this philosophy.

## The Common Language

Throughout the modules, we use a highly specific dialect. Misunderstanding these terms will cause cascading failures in your comprehension.

- **SKU (Stock Keeping Unit):** The lowest level of identification. (e.g., A men's blue linen shirt, size Large).
- **Option / Style:** The design level, ignoring size. (e.g., The men's blue linen shirt). Option count dictates the *width* of your assortment.
- **Depth:** The number of units you buy for a single Option. (**Total Buy = Width × Depth**).
- **ROS (Rate of Sale):** The velocity of a product. Usually measured in units sold per store per week.
- **WOC (Weeks of Cover):** Forward-looking. Based on your current ROS, how many weeks until you run out of stock entirely?
- **Sell-Through %:** Backward-looking. Out of the total units you bought, what percentage have you successfully sold?
- **Markdown:** A deliberate reduction in the selling price, designed to accelerate ROS and liquidate aging stock. 
- **GMROI (Gross Margin Return on Investment):** The ultimate retail metric. For every $1 of capital you invest in inventory, how many $ of gross profit do you get back?

---

## The Core Mathematical Equations

You do not need to memorize these, but you must fundamentally understand the relationships between the variables. If one variable moves, another must react to protect shareholder value.

1. **Gross Margin % = (Net Sales - Cost of Goods Sold) / Net Sales**
2. **Weeks of Cover (WOC) = Current Stock on Hand / Average Weekly Sales**
3. **Sell-Through % = Units Sold / (Units Sold + Current Stock on Hand)**
4. **Stock Turn (Annual) = Annual Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory Value**
5. **GMROI = Gross Margin % × (Sales / Average Inventory Cost)** 
6. **Open-To-Buy (OTB) = Planned Sales + Planned Markdowns + Planned End of Month Inventory - Actual Beginning of Month Inventory - On Order**

## 3. Theoretical Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

### 3.1 Stripping Away the Complexity
At its core, retail solves one problem: **A global manufacturer makes things in bulk. A customer wants one thing, right now. Retail is the system that connects them.**

The manufacturer and the customer do not operate on the same clock, in the same geography, or with the same information. Retail bridges four fundamental gaps:

1. **The Time Gap:** Factories require 6-12 month lead times. Customers decide in 6 seconds. Planning must time capital deployment to bridge this horizon.
2. **The Space Gap:** Production happens in industrial zones globally. Consumption happens locally. 
3. **The Quantity Gap:** Factories optimize for MOQs (Minimum Order Quantities) of 10,000 units. Customers buy 1 unit. Planning breaks bulk without destroying margin.
4. **The Information Gap:** The factory doesn't know what a New York customer wants. The customer doesn't know factory constraints. Planning translates demand signals into production realities.

### 3.2 The Retail Value Chain: From Concept to Cash
Capital flows through the retail engine in a strict sequence:
`CONCEIVE → SOURCE → BUY → RECEIVE → HOLD → ALLOCATE → DISPLAY → SELL → CLEAR → ANALYZE`

Every stage incurs cost or generates risk. The most dangerous stage is **HOLD**. 
Inventory sitting in a distribution center is not an asset; it is a depreciating liability incurring a **Holding Cost**. 

**Mathematical Formulation: The Cost of Holding**
`Annual Holding Cost = Average Inventory Value × Cost of Capital (%)`

*Cost of capital includes weighted average cost of capital (WACC), warehouse leasing, insurance, and shrinkage. In softlines/home, this typically ranges from 12% to 22%.*

If a category averages $70M in inventory at a 15% holding cost, it bleeds $10.5M annually just sitting there. Reducing the holding cycle from 12 months to 8 months unlocks 33% of that trapped cash ($3.46M in pure EBITDA savings).

## 6. Step-by-Step Operational Modeling Lab

**The Scenario:** Merchandising wants to buy 5,000 units of a new Ceramic Vase. Supply chain says the factory MOQ is 10,000 units, which drops the cost from $12 to $10. 

**Step 1: Calculate the 5k Unit Scenario**
- Revenue: 5,000 units × $40 Retail = $200,000
- COGS: 5,000 units × $12 Cost = $60,000
- Initial Margin: $140,000 (70%)

**Step 2: Calculate the 10k Unit MOQ Scenario**
- Revenue: (Assuming demand is only 5,000 full price, the rest must be cleared at 60% off)
- Full Price Rev: 5,000 × $40 = $200,000
- Clearance Rev: 5,000 × $16 = $80,000
- Total Revenue = $280,000
- Total COGS: 10,000 units × $10 Cost = $100,000
- Blended Margin: $180,000 (64.2%)

**Step 3: Apply the System View (The "Hidden" Costs)**
The 10k scenario yields $40k more raw margin, but locks up an extra $40,000 in upfront cash. It incurs 6 months of holding costs in the DC. It cannibalizes space from better-performing SKUs. The Planner's system view rejects the MOQ.

## 7. Post-Mortem: How Retailers Fail

### The "Bullwhip Effect" Autopsy
In 2022, a major US home goods retailer experienced a 5% drop in consumer demand for patio furniture due to unseasonal rain. 

1. **Store Level:** Saw the 5% drop, panicked, and cancelled 10% of their replenishment requests.
2. **Distribution Center:** Saw store orders drop 10%, assumed a trend, and cut warehouse POs by 20%.
3. **Planning & Sourcing:** Saw DC demand drop 20%, assumed a recession, and cut factory production by 40%.
4. **The Result:** When the sun came out in June, demand spiked. The retailer had no inventory. They lost 15% market share to a competitor who understood the system and held strategic buffer stock.

**The Lesson:** Disconnected reactions at localized points in the value chain amplify into catastrophic enterprise failures. Planners must govern the whole chain.

## 3. Theoretical Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

### 3.1 The Revenue Tree (Decomposition)
Net Sales is a lagging indicator. To drive strategy, a planner must decompose revenue into its behavioral drivers.

`Net Sales = Footfall × Conversion Rate × UPT (Units Per Transaction) × ASP (Average Selling Price)`

If sales are flat year-over-year, the Revenue Tree isolates the operational failure:
- Is traffic down? (Marketing issue)
- Is conversion down? (Assortment or staffing issue)
- Is UPT down? (Visual merchandising/cross-selling issue)
- Is ASP down? (Markdown/pricing architecture issue)

### 3.2 The Margin vs. Markup Trap
This is the most dangerous mathematical error in retail. 
- **Markup** is calculated on COST. 
- **Margin** is calculated on SELLING PRICE.

`Markup (%) = (Selling Price - Cost) / Cost`
`Margin (%) = (Selling Price - Cost) / Selling Price`

If a product costs $40 and sells for $100:
- Markup = ($100 - $40) / $40 = **150%**
- Margin = ($100 - $40) / $100 = **60%**

*When a buyer negotiates a "60% markup" with a vendor, they are only achieving a 37.5% margin. This single misunderstanding bankrupts emerging retailers.*

### 3.3 The Margin Waterfall
You cannot take IMU (Initial Markup) to the bank. IMU is what you *hope* to make. Realized Margin is what actually hits the P&L.

`Maintained Margin = IMU - Markdowns - Shrinkage - Employee Discounts`
`Gross Margin ($) = Net Sales - Cost of Goods Sold`

### 3.4 GMROI (Gross Margin Return on Inventory Investment)
The ultimate measure of a planner's effectiveness. It measures how much gross margin is generated for every $1 of inventory capital deployed.

`GMROI = Gross Margin ($) / Average Inventory at Cost ($)`

## 6. Step-by-Step Operational Modeling Lab

**The Scenario:** You must construct the Margin Waterfall for the Q3 Outerwear launch to prove to Finance that a 58% IMU will survive the promotional calendar.

**Step 1: The IMU Baseline**
- MSRP = $120.00
- Landed Cost = $50.40
- **IMU%** = ($120.00 - $50.40) / $120.00 = **58.0%**

**Step 2: Apply the Markdown Budget**
- The promotional calendar dictates an average 12% discount off MSRP across the season.
- Net ASP = $120.00 × 0.88 = $105.60

**Step 3: Apply Shrinkage & Allowances**
- Historical shrink/damage allowance = 2% of Net Sales.
- Net Realized Revenue = $105.60 × 0.98 = $103.49

**Step 4: Calculate Realized Gross Margin**
- Realized Gross Margin ($) = $103.49 - $50.40 = $53.09
- **Realized Gross Margin (%)** = $53.09 / $103.49 = **51.3%**

*Conclusion:* Your 58.0% IMU erodes to a 51.3% Realized Margin. If Finance requires a 52.0% floor, you must either renegotiate the $50.40 cost or reduce the 12% markdown budget.

## 7. Post-Mortem: How Retailers Fail

### Autopsy: The "High Margin / Low Turn" Death Spiral
In 2018, a mid-tier department store fired its Planning Director and allowed Merchandising to allocate 100% of the OTB based purely on IMU%. 
- Buyers flooded the stores with private-label accessories boasting a 75% IMU. 
- However, the product lacked brand pull. Stock Turn plummeted from 3.5x to 1.2x. 
- While the *percentage* margin looked incredible on paper, the *absolute dollars* of gross margin crashed because the velocity of sales halted. 
- The retailer filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy 14 months later with $800M in trapped, aged inventory. 
**The Lesson:** Margin percentage without velocity is an illusion. GMROI is the only truth.

## 3. Theoretical Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

### 3.1 One-Way vs. Two-Way Doors
Not all decisions require the same analytical rigor. The amount of time spent analyzing a decision must be proportional to its irreversibility.
* **One-Way Door (Irreversible):** Signing a $5M factory contract. Requires deep EV modeling, pre-mortems, and consensus.
* **Two-Way Door (Reversible):** Allocating 50 units to a test store. Do not model this. Execute immediately, observe the read, and adjust.

### 3.2 Expected Value (EV) Thinking
Every decision is a bet with shareholder capital. EV quantifies that bet.
`Expected Value = Σ (Outcome × Probability)`

### 3.3 Regret Minimization (The Asymmetric Risk Model)
When probabilities are impossible to know (e.g., launching a radically new category), ask: *"Which decision destroys less enterprise value if I am completely wrong?"*
In retail, the cost of an overbuy (markdown) is usually quantifiable. The cost of an underbuy (lost market share, customer defection) is often existential. 

### 3.4 The 2×2 Constraint Matrix
When facing competing objectives, plot them to visualize the risk.
* **Y-Axis:** Availability (High to Low)
* **X-Axis:** Capital Investment (Low to High)
* *The Goal:* High Availability at Low Investment. *The Reality:* Planners must optimize the trade-off based on the product lifecycle.

```text
CURRENT STATE: 1,300 Units. MSRP $100. (Cost: $40). Trailing Velocity: Slow.

DECISION BRANCH 1: Mark Down NOW (20% Off -> $80)
├── Good Response (70% Prob) -> Sells 600 units -> $48,000 Rev
├── Weak Response (30% Prob) -> Sells 400 units -> $32,000 Rev

DECISION BRANCH 2: Wait 3 Weeks (Full Price), then Mark Down 30% ($70)
├── Full Price Sales (Wait period) -> Sells 180 units -> $18,000 Rev
├── Markdown Sales (50% Prob response) -> Sells 560 units -> $39,200 Rev
└── Expected Value (Revenue): $57,200
```
*Executive Note: Branch 2 yields higher immediate EV, but leaves 560 units of residual stock versus Branch 1. The planner must factor in the terminal write-off risk.*

## 7. Post-Mortem: How Retailers Fail

### Autopsy: The 100% Data Trap (Analysis Paralysis)
In 2021, an international fast-fashion retailer spotted a viral micro-trend on social media. The Merchandising team wanted to immediately inject $2M into chasing the trend. 
The VP of Planning blocked it, demanding 4 weeks of historical sales data to "prove" the trend was mathematically real. 
By the time the 4 weeks passed and the data proved the trend, the 90-day supply chain lead time meant the product would arrive exactly as the trend died. They bought it anyway. It resulted in an $8M write-off.
**The Lesson:** For high-velocity trends, waiting for perfect data is a guarantee of failure. The OODA loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) must spin faster than the market.
