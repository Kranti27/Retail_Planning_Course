## Executive Summary & Core Dilemma

How do we strategically decide exactly which products, in what variety, and at what depth to offer in order to meet consumer demand, maximize enterprise profitability, and ensure optimal inventory productivity across a diverse global store fleet?

---

## What This Is (First Principles Definition)

At its absolute core, **Assortment Planning** is the strategic and mathematical process of defining the product portfolio a retailer will offer. It determines the **Width** (the variety or number of unique options/styles) and the **Depth** (the quantity of units per option). 

Assortment Planning is the architecture of the range. If Demand Forecasting (Module 1.1) projects *how much* revenue we will generate and *how many* total units we require, Assortment Planning translates that macro forecast into the micro-level configuration of actual products on the shelf. 

It is the critical bridge between the creative vision of the merchandising teams and the rigorous mathematical boundaries of financial planning. Planners provide the structural framework (the capital allocation slots), and Buyers/Designers select the actual products to fill those slots. It translates high-level corporate strategy into actionable inventory procurement, ultimately driving enterprise value creation.

---

## Why This Exists (The Problem It Solves)

Without rigorous assortment planning, a retail organization inevitably falls into one of two dangerous extremes, both of which erode shareholder value:

1. **The Over-Assortment Trap:** Procuring too many different styles (high width) with inadequate units per style (low depth). This leads to broken sizing, immediate stockouts on bestsellers, and an inability to display the product properly in-store. Ultimately, it results in lost sales on highly elastic items and margin-destroying markdowns on the fragmented remaining stock.
2. **The Under-Assortment Trap:** Procuring too few styles with massive depth. This leads to a stagnant retail experience, failing to capture nuanced customer segments, and introducing massive markdown risk if those few heavily-invested styles fail to resonate with the market.

Assortment planning exists to solve the fundamental **capital allocation problem**. You have a finite Open-To-Buy (OTB) budget and constrained physical retail square footage. Every SKU (Stock Keeping Unit) you introduce competes for these highly scarce resources. Assortment planning ensures that the portfolio of SKUs is optimized for maximum return on invested capital (ROIC) and maximum revenue realization.

Referencing the Retail Value Chain from Module 0.1:

## The Range Architecture Framework: Good / Better / Best

A healthy assortment must be strategically tiered to capture different demographic segments, address varying price elasticities, and optimize the aggregate margin profile. The standard industry framework for this is Good, Better, Best (G/B/B).

### The G/B/B Price Point Ladder

Let's examine a structured example for an Aura Global Retail sub-category: **Cushion Covers**.

| Tier | Strategic Role | Target ASP ($) | Target Margin % | Assortment Mix (Options) | Sales Mix (Revenue) | Description in Aura Global Retail Context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Good (Entry)** | Traffic Driver, Volume Generator, Market Acquisition | $20 - $30 | 45% - 50% | 20% | 35% | Basic cotton block-print. Simple designs, high velocity. Brings the aspirational consumer into the Aura Global Retail ecosystem. |
| **Better (Core)** | Profit Engine, Core Customer Retention | $40 - $50 | 55% - 60% | 50% | 50% | Intricate hand-block, embroidered cotton/linen blends. The foundation of Aura Global Retail. Balances high volume and robust margin. |
| **Best (Premium)**| Brand Elevator, Margin Builder, Halo Effect | $70 - $100+ | 65%+ | 30% | 15% | Silk brocade, heavy zardozi embroidery. Low velocity, highly decorative. Elevates the brand equity and pricing power of the entire category. |

### How to Decide Price Point Ladders

Price point ladders are never arbitrary. They must be determined by rigorously analyzing historical sales density at various price buckets and identifying the optimum thresholds where price elasticity is minimized. The gap between $25 and $45 must represent a tangible upgrade in perceived consumer value (e.g., superior fabric quality, intricate craftsmanship, premium finishing) to justify the price premium. If the consumer does not perceive this value accretion, the "Better" tier will fail, compressing overall category margins.

### Worked Example: The G/B/B Cascade Calculation

Let us deconstruct exactly how a planner cascades a high-level budget into a precise G/B/B architecture. 

Assume Aura Global Retail has set a seasonal revenue target of **$20 Million** for the Dining category.

**Step 1: Allocate Revenue by Tier**
Based on historical data and strategic enterprise goals, we allocate the revenue mathematically:
- **Good (35%):** $7 Million
- **Better (50%):** $10 Million
- **Best (15%):** $3 Million

**Step 2: Calculate Required Units by Tier**
Using the established Target ASP for each tier:
- **Good Units:** **$7 Million / $25 = 280,000 units**
- **Better Units:** **$10 Million / $45 = 222,222 units**
- **Best Units:** **$3 Million / $80 = 37,500 units**
- **Total Category Units Needed:** **539,722 units**

**Step 3: Define Option Count (Width) Based on Strategy**
We mandate tight curation in "Good" for maximum volume per option, and wider variety in "Best" for visual storytelling and brand elevation.
- **Good Options:** We plan for 10 distinct options.
  - **Depth Calculation:** **280,000 units / 10 = 28,000 units per option depth**
- **Better Options:** We plan for 25 distinct options.
  - **Depth Calculation:** **222,222 units / 25 = 8,889 units per option depth**
- **Best Options:** We plan for 15 distinct options.
  - **Depth Calculation:** **37,500 units / 15 = 2,500 units per option depth**
- **Total Category Options (Width):** 50 options.

This mathematical cascade is non-negotiable. It ensures that the buyer goes to market executing a mandate for exactly 10 "Good" styles, 25 "Better" styles, and 15 "Best" styles, systematically preventing over-assortment and misaligned price architecture.

## Width vs. Depth: The Fundamental Assortment Trade-Off

This is the single most critical mathematical concept in assortment planning and inventory optimization. 

- **Width (Breadth):** The number of unique options (styles, colors, fits) offered.
- **Depth:** The number of units procured per unique option.

Given a fixed capital allocation (OTB) and finite retail square footage, Width and Depth are inversely proportional. You cannot increase one without decreasing the other, absent an injection of new capital.

**Total Units = Width (Options) × Depth (Units per Option)**

### The Critical Minimum Depth

A product cannot generate ROI if it is not displayed properly or if it immediately stocks out of key variants. The **Critical Minimum Depth** is the absolute lowest number of units required per store to make an option commercially viable and visually impactful. It factors in floor display minimums, backroom buffer requirements, and supply chain lead times.

**Worked Calculation:**
Imagine you are planning Aura Global Retail's Hardgoods category.
- **OTB Budget for Sub-Category:** $10 Million
- **Target ASP (Average Selling Price):** $50
- **Total Units Affordable:** **$10 Million / $50 = 200,000 units**
- **Store Count:** 250 stores

**Scenario A: The Over-Assortment Trap (High Width)**
The design team proposes a massive, fragmented range of 250 options to maximize "newness."
- **Depth per Option:** **200,000 units / 250 options = 800 units per option**
- **Units per Store per Option:** **800 units / 250 stores = 3.2 units per store per option**
*Result:* Mathematically ruinous. You cannot allocate 0.2 units to a store. Even with store clustering, most locations receive zero, and those that receive product get 1 unit. That 1 unit acts as a lone display piece, sells out instantly, and leaves an unfillable void on the floor. This severely degrades working capital efficiency and ROIC.

**Scenario B: The Optimized Range (Balanced Width/Depth)**
You enforce a minimum presentation requirement of 3 units per store to maintain brand standards and cover supply chain latency.
- **Required Depth per Option:** **3 units × 250 stores = 750 units minimum**
- **Maximum Allowable Options (Width):** **200,000 total units / 750 units per option = 266 options (rounded down)**
*Result:* You push back on the design team's proposal. The mandate is restricted to 16 options. The range must be highly curated, but every single option will be backed by sufficient inventory depth to capture demand and generate sustainable enterprise revenue.

## Fixture & Space Capacity Constraints

Financial capital (OTB) is only one half of the constraint equation. The other half is rigid physical infrastructure: **Store Space**.

A retail store operates within a finite square footage, translating into a finite number of display fixtures. Each fixture has a mathematical maximum capacity based on product dimensions. Over-assorting beyond physical capacity traps surplus product in the backroom (yielding zero ROI) while tying up valuable working capital and impairing store operations.

### The Physics of Assortment

1. **Calculate Linear Capacity:** A standard wall bay is 3 feet wide with 5 shelves. That yields 15 linear feet of display space per bay.
2. **Calculate Option Density:** If a folded quilt requires 1.5 linear feet for optimal presentation, one shelf holds exactly 2 options. A 5-shelf bay holds exactly 10 options.
3. **Establish Category Caps:** If Aura Global Retail allocates 8 wall bays to the Quilts category, the absolute maximum option count the business can physically display is **8 bays × 10 options = 80 options**.

**The Zero-Sum Strategy:**
Because retail space is finite, assortment planning is a zero-sum capital allocation exercise. If the buyer intends to introduce 5 new styles mid-season to capture an emerging trend, they MUST identify 5 existing styles to mark down, liquidate, and remove from the floor. You cannot force 85 options into an 80-option capacity without degrading the visual merchandising standard and creating a chaotic, brand-diluting customer experience.

## Store-Level Assortment Tiering (A/B/C Grading)

It is a profound operational failure to deploy an identical assortment width across the entire store fleet. Stores possess divergent square footages, traffic volumes, and demographic profiles. We optimize this variance through rigorous **Store Tiering**.

Stores are classified based on revenue volume and physical footprint into A, B, and C tiers. The assortment width must flex dynamically to match the tier's capacity and risk profile.

### The Tiering Strategy

- **"A" Grade Stores (Flagships / High Volume / Premium Real Estate):**
  - Receive the **Full Width** (100% of the planned options).
  - Merchandised with the complete G/B/B cascade, including all low-velocity, high-priced "Best" tier statement pieces to project brand authority.
  - Backed by high depth to support massive foot traffic and superior inventory turns.
  - Example: A 10,000 sq ft flagship in a tier-one market receives all 250 category options.

- **"B" Grade Stores (Standard / Mall / High Street):**
  - Receive an **Edited Width** (e.g., 70% of the planned options).
  - Merchandised with the full "Good" and "Better" tiers, but only a curated, risk-averse selection of the "Best" tier.
  - Eliminated options are typically fringe colors, extreme sizes, or highly volatile fashion styles.
  - Example: A standard 5,000 sq ft store receives 175 of the 250 options.

- **"C" Grade Stores (Small Footprint / Regional / Value-Conscious):**
  - Receive a **Core-Only Width** (e.g., 40% of the planned options).
  - Merchandised with heavily localized "Good" and "Better" tier staples. Almost entirely stripped of high-risk "Best" and volatile fashion inventory to protect margin.
  - Maximizes depth on proven, high-velocity winners to ensure high ROS (Rate of Sale) in constrained space.
  - Example: A compact 2,500 sq ft regional store receives only 100 core options.

By systematically tiering the assortment, executives ensure that working capital is not trapped in slow-moving "Best" tier inventory stagnating in small "C" stores where the local demographic lacks the purchasing power to support premium price points.

## Core vs. Fashion vs. Basic Matrix

Not all products behave uniformly or carry the same enterprise risk. The assortment must be systematically classified by its lifecycle and risk profile, which dictates strict capital allocation strategies.

| Classification | % of Range (Volume) | Risk Level | Lifecycle | Planning Strategy | Aura Global Retail Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Basics / Continuity** | 60% - 70% | Low | 12 - 24+ Months | Algorithmic replenishment. Never out of stock. High depth, low width. Protects base revenue. | Standard white/ecru cotton bedsheets; classic wooden trays. |

## Customer Decision Tree (CDT) & The Paradox of Choice

The **Customer Decision Tree (CDT)** maps the psychological hierarchy of criteria a consumer utilizes when making a purchase decision. Executives leverage the CDT to ensure the assortment offers robust optionality where it drives conversion, and eliminates costly redundancy where it does not.

*Example CDT for an Aura Global Retail Customer buying Bed Linen:*
1. **Category:** Bedding
2. **Sub-Category:** Bedsheets (vs. Quilts)
3. **Size:** King (vs. Queen/Single) — *Functional Constraint, must offer complete coverage.*
4. **Fabric/Feel:** Percale Cotton vs. Sateen — *Comfort Preference.*
5. **Price Tier:** Better ($50) vs. Best ($90) — *Budget Constraint.*
6. **Design/Color:** Indigo Block Print vs. Pastel Solid — *Aesthetic Choice.*

**Assortment Implication:** The assortment architecture must provide adequate, unbroken choices at the upper nodes (Size and Price Tier) without overwhelming the consumer at the terminal node (Design/Color). 

**The Paradox of Choice:** Offering 50 marginally different shades of blue bedsheets does not aggregate incremental sales; it induces decision paralysis. A tight, highly curated range of 5 distinct, high-conviction blue designs will yield superior conversion rates, accelerated purchase decisions, and dramatically higher inventory turns.

## Assortment Productivity Analysis

How do executives definitively audit whether an assortment architecture successfully drove value? We measure the financial productivity of every single "slot" in the range.

### Key Metrics

- **Revenue per Option:** **Total Sales / Number of Options**
- **Margin per Option:** **Total Gross Margin $ / Number of Options**
- **Rate of Sale (ROS):** Units sold per option per store per week.

### The Long Tail Problem & ABC Analysis

In almost every loosely managed assortment, the Pareto Principle (the 80/20 rule) applies aggressively, severely diluting enterprise returns.

- **A-Class SKUs (Top 20%):** Generate 70-80% of total revenue.
- **B-Class SKUs (Middle 30%):** Generate 15-20% of total revenue.
- **C-Class SKUs (Bottom 50% - "The Long Tail"):** Generate a marginal 5-10% of revenue, yet consume disproportionate retail space, supply chain bandwidth, and critical working capital.

**The Sarah Playbook (SKU Rationalization):**
When Sarah, VP of Merchandising, assumed leadership of a $1 Billion category at Aura Global Retail, she identified a bloated option count of 2,500. A rigorous ABC analysis revealed that the bottom 1,000 SKUs (40% of the total width) contributed a mere 6% of sales, yet tied up 25% of the division's working capital and cluttered flagship fixtures. 

*Action:* Sarah ruthlessly executed a 20% SKU rationalization, immediately liquidating 500 of the lowest-yielding options. She reallocated that freed-up capital directly into increasing the *depth* of the top 200 "A-Class" SKUs, guaranteeing zero stockouts on top revenue drivers during peak trading. 
*Result:* Total category revenue expanded by 15%, and inventory turns accelerated by 30%. This margin expansion was achieved without increasing the aggregate budget, driven entirely by enforcing per-SKU productivity thresholds and amputating the long tail.

## The Range Planning Process

The chronological governance workflow for architecting the assortment:

1. **Post-Mortem / Strategic Review:** What generated ROI? What destroyed margin? Execution of ABC analysis to identify targeted SKU rationalization opportunities.
2. **Space & Capital Allocation:** Finance issues the OTB capital budget; Retail Operations confirms fixture capacities and updates the store grading matrix.
3. **The Strategic Brief:** Planners architect the quantitative framework (e.g., "Mandate: 15 Good options, 25 Better, 10 Best. 60% Core, 40% Seasonal").
4. **Option Selection (The Buy):** Merchandisers and Designers curate the physical product samples that conform to the exact financial slots defined in the brief.
5. **Executive Sign-Off & Quantification:** Planners assign the precise depth (units) to each approved option based on projected ROS, price elasticity, and store tiering distribution models.

## Strategic Trade-Offs & Risk Matrices

- **Capital Efficiency vs. Visual Impact:** Planners advocate for high depth and low width to maximize capital efficiency (minimizing markdown risk, maximizing in-stock rates). Visual Merchandising teams lobby for high width to create a vibrant, visually dense retail environment. David must negotiate the exact point of equilibrium that optimizes shareholder value.
- **Risk Mitigation vs. Trend Leadership:** Heavily weighting the portfolio toward Basics and Core minimizes immediate financial risk but erodes brand relevance over the long term. A calculated capital allocation to High-Risk Fashion (capped at 5-10%) is a necessary "R&D" investment to sustain Aura Global Retail's market leadership, acknowledging the inherently higher markdown rates on these trend items.
- **Localization vs. Standardization:** Customizing the assortment for every individual store maximizes local market share but introduces massive friction and administrative bloat across the supply chain, allocation teams, and visual merchandisers. Store grading (A/B/C) provides an optimized, scalable equilibrium between hyper-localization and rigid corporate standardization.

## Strategic & Operational Pitfalls

1. **Adding Width Without Capital Injections (The "Peanut Butter" Approach):** Approving a merchandising request to add 20 new styles without a corresponding increase in the OTB budget. This forces the planner to spread the exact same capital thinner, diluting depth across the entire portfolio and mathematically guaranteeing stockouts on top revenue drivers.
2. **Ignoring Cannibalization:** Launching a new SKU that heavily overlaps with a current bestseller, under the flawed assumption it will yield purely incremental revenue. In reality, it fractures existing demand, doubling the working capital requirement to achieve the exact same top-line result.
3. **Failing to Amputate the Tail (Emotional Attachment):** Retaining a C-class product in the portfolio due to "sentimental value" or design team preference, in direct defiance of empirical data proving the market rejects it. This destroys ROIC.
4. **Copy-Pasting Historical Architectures:** Lazily rolling over the previous year's assortment structure without recalibrating for shifting consumer price elasticity, macroeconomic inflation, or structural changes in the retail footprint.
5. **Planning Averages, Not Stores:** Evaluating only aggregate company depth (e.g., "We procured 1,000 units, we are covered") without dividing by the 300 stores in the fleet to realize it equates to a commercially unviable 3.3 units per store.

Calculate the precise unit procurement required for each tier.
2. **Width vs. Depth Capital Allocation:** You manage an OTB budget of $2.5 Million for a capsule collection. The target ASP is $1,000. Retail operations mandates a minimum presentation depth of 5 units per store across a 150-store fleet to uphold visual standards. What is the absolute maximum option count (width) you can authorize for the design team?
3. **Fixture Constraint Analysis:** A category is allocated 12 wall bays across the fleet. Each bay holds exactly 15 unique options. ## Key Takeaways

1. **Assortment Planning is Capital Allocation:** It is the rigorous mathematical process of defining the width (options) and depth (units) of a product portfolio to maximize return on invested capital within rigid budget and spatial constraints.
2. **Width vs. Depth is Inversely Proportional:** Under a fixed capital constraint, expanding the number of options mathematically necessitates a reduction in units per option. Executives must fiercely defend the Critical Minimum Depth to ensure commercial viability.
3. **Space is a Zero-Sum Constraint:** Physical store fixtures impose absolute limits on option capacity. Over-assorting beyond this capacity traps working capital in backrooms where it yields zero return.
4. **Store Tiering Maximizes Efficiency:** Fleet-wide assortment uniformity is an operational failure. Tiering stores (A/B/C) ensures low-volume locations are not burdened by slow-moving, margin-diluting inventory.
5. **G/B/B Tiering Controls Enterprise Margins:** A disciplined Good/Better/Best architecture ensures the brand captures entry-level market share while aggressively defending aggregate margins through premium tiers.
6. **Amputate the Tail Aggressively:** The bottom 30-50% of an assortment typically generates negligible revenue while consuming massive corporate resources. Routine SKU rationalization is non-negotiable for sustaining high inventory productivity.
7. **Beware Cannibalization:** Introducing similar options at identical price points rarely scales top-line revenue; it fractures existing demand, decelerates inventory turns, and exacerbates markdown risk.
