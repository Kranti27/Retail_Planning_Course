**Level:** Core

## Executive Summary & Core Dilemma

How do we translate a theoretical financial and assortment plan into physical store space, ensuring that the maximum sales and margin are generated from every square foot of real estate without violating visual merchandising guidelines?

---

## What This Is (First Principles Definition)

**Space Planning** is the analytical process of determining how much physical space (in square feet or fixture count) should be allocated to different categories, sub-categories, or items within a store to maximize the return on that real estate.

**Planogramming (POG)** is the tactical translation of space planning. A Planogram is a visual blueprint or schematic that dictates exactly where and how merchandise should be displayed on a specific fixture. It defines the number of options (facings) and the depth of stock per option, thereby defining the physical capacity of the fixture.

From first principles: Retail is essentially a real estate business. You lease square footage at a fixed cost and attempt to push as much profitable inventory through that space as possible over time. The physical capacity acts as a hard boundary on all planning equations. 

---

## Why This Exists (The Problem It Solves)

Planners operate in spreadsheets where they can infinitely expand assortment width (number of options) and depth (units per option). However, physical reality hits when the goods arrive at the store. 

If space planning is ignored:
- **Overcrowding:** Stores become cluttered bazaars. Visual aesthetics degrade, destroying brand equity. 
- **Stockouts on Floor:** If the physical shelf can only hold 2 units, but the weekly rate of sale is 10 units, the store staff must restock 5 times a week from the backroom. If they forget, the item shows as a stockout to the customer, even though the store holds inventory.
- **Lost Margin:** Highly profitable, fast-selling categories might be starved for space, while slow-moving, bulky items take up prime real estate.

Space planning bridges the gap between the theoretical financial targets (Sales, Margin, Inventory) and the physical realities of the retail floor.

---

## How It Connects to the Retail System

Referencing the Retail Value Chain (Module 0.1):
- **Assortment Planning (Module 1.2):** Space dictates the maximum assortment width. You cannot plan for 150 cushion cover options if the store fixtures can only display 80.
- **Buying (Module 1.3):** OTB budgets must factor in the visual minimums (the stock required just to make the store look full).
- **Allocation (Module 4.2):** The Initial Store Quantity (ISQ) calculated must be validated against fixture capacity.

Space planning is the physical bottleneck through which all preceding plans must pass.

## Space Productivity Metrics (The Calculations)

How do we measure if space is being used effectively? We rely on three primary metrics.

### A. Sales per Square Foot (SPSF)
The most common real estate metric. It measures top-line revenue generated by a physical area.

**SPSF = (Total Net Sales) / (Total Square Footage)**

### B. Gross Margin Return on Footage (GMROF)
Sales are vanity, margin is sanity. GMROF tells you how much gross profit is generated per square foot. It is the ultimate equalizer between categories with different margins and space requirements.

**GMROF = (Total Gross Margin ($)) / (Total Square Footage)**

### C. Density (Units per Square Foot)
Measures how much inventory is packed into the space. High density means more stock on the floor; low density means a sparser, more premium look.

**Density = (Total Units on Display) / (Total Square Footage)**

### Worked Example: Aura Global Retail Categories

Sarah manages the Home & Lifestyle category, specifically Hardgoods (Furniture) and Softgoods (Table Linen, Bedding). Let's compare their space productivity in a 2,000 sqft Home section of an Experience Center.

| Metric | Furniture (Hardgoods) | Table Linen (Softgoods) |
|--------|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| Annual Sales | $2.5 Million | $1.2 Million |
| Gross Margin % | 45% | 65% |
| Gross Margin ($) | $1.12 Million | $780,000 |
| Space Allocated (sqft) | 1,400 sqft | 200 sqft |
| Units on Floor | 150 units | 2,500 units |

**Calculations for Furniture:**
- SPSF = 2,50,00,000 / 1,400 = $17,857/ sqft
- GMROF = 1,12,50,000 / 1,400 = $8,035/ sqft
- Density = 150 / 1,400 = 0.11 units / sqft

**Calculations for Table Linen:**
- SPSF = 1,20,00,000 / 200 = $60,000/ sqft
- GMROF = 78,00,000 / 200 = $39,000/ sqft
- Density = 2,500 / 200 = 12.5 units / sqft

**Analysis:**
Furniture generates huge top-line revenue, but because it is incredibly bulky (low density), its return on space (GMROF of $8,035) is vastly inferior to Table Linen (GMROF of $39,000). Table Linen is a space-productivity powerhouse. However, Furniture often acts as an "anchor" or "magnet" category that draws customers in, who then impulsively buy the highly profitable Table Linen.

## Space Allocation Math

How do we decide how much space a category deserves? The fundamental rule of thumb is:

**Space Share % should roughly equal Sales Share % or Margin Share %.**

If a category's Space Share > Sales Share, it is "over-spaced."
If a category's Space Share < Sales Share, it is "under-spaced."

### Worked Example: Re-allocating Space

Imagine a 1,000 sqft Home Décor section. The current allocation is:

| Category | Current Sales ($) | Sales Share % | Current Space (sqft) | Space Share % | Status |
|----------|-------------------|---------------|----------------------|---------------|--------|
| Cushions | $400,000 | 40% | 200 sqft | 20% | Under-spaced (highly productive) |
| Rugs | $300,000 | 30% | 500 sqft | 50% | Over-spaced (drag on GMROF) |
| Wall Art | $300,000 | 30% | 300 sqft | 30% | Parity |
| **Total** | **$1 Million** | **100%** | **1,000 sqft** | **100%** | |

**The Action:** 
Cushions are generating 40% of the sales from only 20% of the space. Sarah should consider expanding the Cushion section and shrinking Rugs.

**The Space Elasticity of Demand:**
Wait! If we double the space for Cushions from 200 to 400 sqft, will sales double to $800,000? 
**No.** This is the Space Elasticity of Demand. Usually, giving a category more space gives diminishing returns. Giving Cushions 20% more space might only yield 5% more sales, but it will improve visual presentation and reduce out-of-stocks on the floor. Space reallocation must be iterative.

## Planograms (POGs) & Fixture Capacity

A Planogram is the atomic unit of space planning. It looks at the individual fixture (e.g., a wall bay or a mid-floor nesting table).

**Key POG Concepts:**
- **Facing (Width):** The number of visible units of a single SKU at the front of a shelf. 
- **Depth:** How many units of that same SKU are stacked behind or under the facing.
- **Fixture Capacity:** Facings × Depth

### Worked Example: Fixture Capacity Math

Aura Global Retail uses a standard "Wall Bay Fixture" for displaying Cushion Covers. 
- The fixture has 4 shelves.
- Each shelf can comfortably fit 5 facings (options).
- Each facing can be stacked 6 units deep.

**Total Options (Width) per fixture:** 4 shelves × 5 facings = 20 options.
**Total Capacity (Units) per fixture:** 20 options × 6 depth = 120 units.

If a store has 3 of these Wall Bays allocated to Cushions:
- **Maximum Assortment Width:** 20 × 3 = 60 options.
- **Maximum Visual Capacity:** 120 × 3 = 360 units.

If the Assortment Planner bought 80 options for this store tier, the plan is broken. The store physically cannot display 20 of those options. They will sit in the backroom, unseen and unsold.

---

## The Visual Merchandising (VM) vs. Planning Tension

There is a permanent, structural tension between the Planning team and the Visual Merchandising (VM) team.

**The Planner's View:** Maximize density. Pack the shelves. If we have 120 capacity, let's put 120 units out. High density means high GMROF, fewer lost sales, and less backroom storage required.
**The VM View:** Maximize aesthetics. Create negative space. Let the product "breathe" to elevate Aura Global Retail perception from a mass-market bazaar to a premium boutique. VM might decree that only 3 units deep is allowed, cutting the fixture capacity in half.

**The Solution: Forward Stock vs. Backroom Stock**
To bridge this gap, retail separates inventory into:
- **Forward Stock:** The inventory visibly displayed on the fixture according to VM guidelines.
- **Backroom Stock (BOH - Back of House):** Additional inventory kept in the stockroom to replenish the Forward Stock as it sells.

If VM restricts the cushion depth to 3 units (cutting display capacity from 360 to 180), but the planner knows the weekly rate of sale is 200 units, the remaining units must be stored in the backroom, and store operations must rigorously replenish the floor daily.

---

## Store Capacity as an Allocation Constraint

In Module 4.2, we learned about the Initial Store Quantity (ISQ). But ISQ is a theoretical math output. It must be constrained by physics.

The formula for maximum allocation to a store is:
**Max Allocation = Forward POG Capacity + Backroom Capacity Limit**

### Worked Example: The Allocation Constraint

- **SKU:** Hand-painted Ceramic Vase
- **Calculated ISQ (from Sales Plan):** 40 units
- **POG Directive:** The vase goes on a nesting table. VM allows 2 facings, 2 deep. 
  - Forward Capacity = 4 units.
- **Backroom Constraint:** The vase is fragile and bulky. The store manager confirms the maximum safe backroom space for this item is 10 units.
  - Max Allocation = 4 (Forward) + 10 (Backroom) = 14 units.

**Conflict:** The planner's math says send 40 units. The physical constraint says send 14 units.
**Resolution:** The planner must send 14 units and rely on frequent, high-velocity Replenishment (Module 4.3) rather than a large upfront allocation.

## Assortment Localization by Store Size

Stores are not identically sized. You cannot force a flagship assortment into an express store. Assortment width must be truncated based on physical capacity.

### The Aura Global Retail Store Tiers:

| Store Tier | Size | Assortment Width | Strategy |
|------------|------|------------------|----------|
| **Flagship** | 10,000 sqft | 100% ('Best') | Carries the full matrix, including large furniture, premium collections, and high-risk fashion items. |
| **Standard** | 5,000 sqft | 60% ('Better') | Carries core items plus top-performing seasonal/fashion pieces. Drops large furniture. |
| **Express** | 1,500 sqft | 30% ('Good/Core')| Highly curated. Only high-density, high-GMROF items (linen, personal care, small decor). |

When David signs off on an assortment plan, he ensures that the Option Count matches the Fixture Count for each specific store grade.

## Strategic Trade-Offs & Risk Matrices

| Decision | Optimize for High Density (Planner) | Optimize for Aesthetics (VM) |
|----------|-------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Pros** | Maximizes sales/sqft. Reduces out-of-stocks on floor. Lowers holding costs in warehouse. | Premium brand perception. Better customer experience. Highlights key items. |
| **Cons** | Can look messy, cheap, and overwhelming. Harder for customers to shop. | Requires pristine backroom management. High risk of lost sales if floor staff don't restock instantly. |
| **Framework** | High density for Core/Never-Out-of-Stock items (e.g., plain napkins, basic candles). | Low density for high-ticket, premium, or new seasonal launches (e.g., hand-carved console table). |

---

## Strategic & Operational Pitfalls

1. **Planning Assortment Width in a Vacuum:** Buying 200 options when the total store fixture capacity across all grades maxes out at 150 options. The remaining 50 options are doomed to fail.
2. **Ignoring Backroom Capacity Constraints:** Focusing only on POG capacity and assuming the backroom is infinitely elastic. It's not.
3. **Equating Space Elasticity to 1:1:** Believing that doubling a category's space will double its sales. It almost never does.
4. **Using Only Sales to Allocate Space:** Allocating space based purely on revenue, ignoring Margin and physical volume (bulkiness). This ruins GMROF.
5. **The "Set It and Forget It" POG:** Failing to update Planograms as seasonality shifts (e.g., not expanding Diya/Candle space ahead of Diwali).
